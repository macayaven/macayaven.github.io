Ikigai Loop: an AI assistant I'm running on myself

2 November 2026 · Meta's Muse models, agent evals, a live run on my own data

Ikigai Loop is an AI assistant that suggests, five times a day, how to spend my next free hour, on my work goals or with my family, and that never shows me a number it can't back with my own records. I'm running it on myself from 2 to 4 November 2026, for Meta's hackathon.

It looks at what I actually did, from my screen time, my calendar and my public GitHub activity. It picks two options from a list of promises I wrote down myself and sends them to my phone as a Telegram card. Before a card goes out, plain code checks every figure in it against those records, and a wrong one is sent back. I choose with a tap, or correct the card with a voice note.

How it works

  • Signals: screen time from ActivityWatch on my Mac, my two calendars and my public GitHub activity.
  • Facts: code turns those records into numbers for three 24-hour periods ending at the card's hour, called earlier, prior and last. Muse Glimmer, running on my own DGX Spark, adds facts of its own, each citing the records it came from.
  • Proposals: Muse Wonder gets only those facts and my written commitments, and proposes one commitment per goal for my next free hour, with its reasons.
  • The gate: plain code checks every number and source in those reasons before the card is sent. A wrong draft gets one retry; if the retry fails too, I get a labelled fallback with no reasons.
  • Me: I tap Take A, Take B or Neither now, or send a voice note to correct the card.

The gate checks numbers, sources and wording against my records. It doesn't check that a sentence is true. The misses so far, and what it still doesn't check, are on the trust page.

Muse Code wrote the assistant's code, with the exceptions listed in the repo's CONTRIBUTIONS.md. The code and its evidence are at github.com/macayaven/ikigai-loop.

The gate's checks also run in your browser, as a game: Catch the AI. Muse Wonder wrote six sentences about everyday clutter and set traps in some. Can you beat the checker?

Field notes

Newest first. Each note is one real thing the build or the run showed, with its evidence.

4 November, the checker: what it checks, and how it changed

The checker is the part of Ikigai Loop anyone can try. The page shows a card of numbers and a sentence an AI wrote about them. The card is the truth; the sentence is what gets checked.

How it works: plain code, with no model, finds each number in the sentence and matches it to a card row by that row's own words. Then it compares the two. A sentence that says 12 deploys when the card says 7 fails. A number that matches no row fails too, so a sentence that renames a row fails safe.

Why it exists: assistants quote your data back to you, get some numbers wrong, and sound just as sure. On my own cards, the same check stops a wrong number before the card reaches my phone. The page runs that Python in your browser, so you can try the idea with nothing to set up. On public data with known answers, it stopped 120 of 120 planted errors and passed 38 of 38 true claims (domains).

How it changed:

  • 1 November: a command line and a first browser page that took JSON (#19).
  • 2 November: a form a stranger can fill in without JSON (#33). The card miss in the note below, and the rule that came from it (#43). The gate's hardening, brought into the page (#51).
  • 3 November: Catch the AI, a game where Muse Wonder hides traps in six sentences (#61). A consented version for a paid study, with its plan registered first (#64).
  • 4 November: a first-time user couldn't tell which side was the truth, and the line about the checker's limits read as generic. Now the page says the card is the truth, and each result says what it was checked against (#75).

Known limit: it compares numbers with the card's names, nothing more. It can't tell whether a sentence is true. In the plain form it doesn't check units, time windows or words like rose and fell. One game round puts the right number in the wrong season, and the checker passes it, on purpose.

4 November, demo cut: the recording needs checks too

The first demo cut is 2 minutes 56 seconds long. It shows a voice correction, a card reaching my phone, and the wrong-period failure from the notes below. The checker game stays in too, including a trap the checker misses.

The narration is my voice, cloned locally with Chatterbox from my own recording. I disclosed it on the end card and in the video description. The footage also distinguishes the real run, made-up tests and a recorded replay. A sentence rejected in a replay is evidence about the fixed gate; it doesn't undo the sentence that reached my phone.

What I changed: I put this cut up as unlisted and made a first hackathon submission. That leaves me time to finish the recording before the deadline. I'm keeping revisions in the build that produces the video, so the final cut can be rebuilt and checked for private data.

What still needs work: the phone and checker need more of the frame, the text cards need less text, and the editing labels need to go. The live-run placeholders need the frozen figures from the run's report. The failure and the checker's miss stay.

Known limit: my voice tests also used the clone. In my audio evaluation, 50 of 60 edits came out right from audio, against 58 of 60 from text. Every audio miss asked a question instead of making a wrong edit. Those were clean synthetic takes, with no room noise, phone microphone or Telegram codec. I still need a broader test with voice notes recorded on the phone, including mixed English and Spanish.

3 November, 11:30 card: "since the last card", on a real card

This morning's card was the first to use yesterday's fix. Its first draft passed the gate, and its Why? reply ended with this sentence:

Prs rose by 11 since the last card.

That's the shape the rule asks for. Yesterday at 15:30, the same kind of change came out as "Prs rose by 3 in the last period", and the gate let it through.

The rule went live at 23:29 on 2 November. Its go/no-go ran all 54 made-up cases: 2 first drafts failed the gate, and both passed on the retry.

A second rehearsal that night used a copy of my data and my real list of private terms. The new gate stopped ordinary card sentences as if they held a private term. The 11:30 card fell back to a plain card in 5 runs out of 5.

What I changed: a private-term match made only of card words no longer counts. An exact match of the whole term still does. The same rehearsal then fell back in none of 5 runs, and the fix went live at 01:27, before any card used the 23:29 gate. It's merged in pull request #58.

Known limit: a private term made only of card words, written as separate words, isn't caught. And the gate checks the 11 against my records at card time; a separate audit recounts it from GitHub later.

2 November, 15:30 card: the right number, the wrong period

The second real card passed the gate with this sentence:

Prs rose by 3 in the last period.

The 3 was right. The sentence was wrong. On the same card, another sentence said pull requests were 27 in the last period, against 2 in the prior one: from the prior period to the last, they rose by 25.

The 3 was the change since my previous card, at 13:30. Then the last period held 24 pull requests; at 15:30 it held 27. The gate checked that 3 matched the stored change since the last card, and that the sentence named the last period. "In the last period" names it, so the sentence passed.

What I changed: first a test that fails on that exact sentence, then a rule. A change since the last card has to say "since the last card", and a phrase that puts the change inside a period is rejected. Muse Wonder is now taught that phrase, so cards don't start failing: on ten made-up cases, all ten drafts passed, and every since-last sentence said "since the last card". The fix is merged in pull request #43. It reaches the live cards on 3 November only if that night's checks pass.

Known limit: the new rule also rejects one true but clumsy shape, "Pushes in the last period increased by 1 since the last card". The taught prompt wrote it in none of 252 claims.
